Health workers judge this by eye, on over a billion labels a year. VialSight measures it, applies the WHO rule, and shows the number behind every call.

    How it reads a label

    No AI model makes the decision. Image processing finds and measures the label, and an exact rule decides.

    1. FindContours and an ellipse fit locate every outer circle in the frame, at any size or rotation.
    2. StraightenEach ellipse is resampled into a front-on circle, which undoes the camera's tilt.
    3. MeasurePixels move to CIELAB, a colour space built on human lightness. The square's centre is compared with the circle's ring.
    4. Self-calibrateThe circle is the label's own reference colour. A fitted plane across it cancels uneven light, so no colour card is needed.
    5. DecideWHO rule: discard when the square is the same as or darker than the circle. Glare, blur, tilt or an unstable reading asks for a rescan instead of a guess.

    Accuracy, measured

    Synthetic trays with known ground truth: every square is set to a known lightness offset from its circle, then hit with uneven light, colour cast, tilt, rotation, glare, noise and blur. Run it yourself.

    Why not just ask an AI?

    We asked a state-of-the-art vision-language model, Qwen3-VL-30B, the same question on the same labels. Reading a few lightness units of difference is a measurement, not a description.

    Limits, honestly

    • Decision support for trained staff, not a replacement for them. When unsure, it asks for a rescan.
    • A VVM shows cumulative heat. It does not show freezing damage; the shake test still applies.
    • Validated here on synthetic labels and WHO reference artwork. A field trial on real vials is the next step.
    • The USE FIRST band is a field convention. WHO defines only the discard point, which VialSight applies exactly.
